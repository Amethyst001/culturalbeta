## Abstract reasoning: what is actually being measured, and what would falsify it

The entry makes one causal-adjacent claim dressed as a measurement claim: **a consumer stock's weekly return variance has a component attributable to a taste factor constructed from Qloo, and that factor adds information beyond (a) the S&P 500 and (b) the holding's own sector peers' committed prices.**

Let me stress-test that claim at the level of its moving parts before I look at execution.

**What is the instrument?** The taste factor is `ln(fold-change in trending velocity)` of a per-sub-sector basket of Qloo recommendations (brand/movie/artist/tv_show, affinity-weighted, universe-excluded). So the "regressor" is a weekly scalar derived from a social-graph trending metric. The claim is that this scalar, fitted by OLS against SPY, predicts residual weekly stock return. That is a very specific structural assertion: that a *change in the cultural attention of a generic sub-sector audience* moves the *price* of a specific issuer.

**What would falsify it?** A negative out-of-sample ΔR² after adding taste, or — the harder falsification — a negative *ablation* (peer basket before taste, OOS peer-only vs peer+taste). The entry correctly identifies the ablation as the real test, because the peer basket already carries most of the sector-level taste exposure. A positive reading survives only if taste adds over peers. The entry is, genuinely, built to print the negative. That is the intellectually honest design and it is the strongest thing in the piece.

**Does the instrument, estimator, and control establish the claim?**
- The instrument is defensible but it has a confound the entry half-acknowledges: trending-velocity is an *attention/velocity* measure, not a *demand/valuation* measure. A stock can react to a taste trend that is not economically material, or to a taste trend that is already fully priced by the sector. The OOS ΔR² over a 36-week block (≈42 observations, with 8-week circular blocks giving few independent effective draws) has the interval the entry now reports — and the entry's own stub run shows the primary interval *crosses zero*. So on the data they can currently run, the instrument does **not** establish the claim; it is a null-leaning point estimate. The provision (real keyed data) may move the point, but it cannot move the *design*: with 106 weeks and a 36-week OOS holdout, the effective sample for an out-of-sample R² test of a single added regressor is small. A provision cannot buy statistical power that the window length doesn't have.
- The control (peer basket) is correct in concept but the entry's framing that "peers carry the same taste exposure, so a positive reading is a lower bound" is actually the opposite of what a lower-bound claim requires. If peers already absorb the sector taste, then taste-over-peers is measuring the *non-overlapping, name-specific* taste layer, and a negative reading means "Qloo adds nothing over what you can build from committed prices." That is exactly the "what is this worth if negative" case, and the entry handles it. But the phrase "lower bound" undersells the result: a negative ablation is not a conservative estimate of a real positive effect, it is a finding that the effect is plausibly zero. The copy should say "null" not "lower bound."
- The multiplicity handling (BH across 101 |t|, flag needs sign + |t|≥2 + q≤0.10) is correct and appropriately harsh. Good.

**What is the product worth if negative?** This is where I think the entry is actually stronger than the numbers suggest, and it's the core of my score. If the ablation is negative, the deliverable is: (1) a pre-registered, reproducible, BH-corrected negative result — genuinely citable by a research desk because it was fixed before the key; (2) a *vendor-free* sector-peer exposure model that a quant can run on committed prices alone; and (3) an alerts feed where "no alert" is inspectable (suppressed names + reasons). The entry designs the null branch as a card, not an apology. That is the right product posture for a measurement instrument. The value in the negative case is real but it is *research/infrastructure* value, not *trading-signal* value, and the entry is honest about the no-trading line.

So abstractly: the claim is narrow, falsifiable, and the design survives its own falsification. The main risk is that the statistical power at 106 weeks is thin for a single-regressor OOS test, and the entry's own intervals flag that honestly.

## Abstract reasoning: what is actually being measured, and what would falsify it

Strip the entry to its skeleton: **a consumer stock's weekly return has a component explained by a taste regressor (ln fold-change in Qloo trending velocity, per-sub-sector basket), and that regressor adds out-of-sample explanatory power over (a) the S&P 500 and (b) the holding's own committed-price sector peers.**

Three moving parts, stress-tested:

**The instrument.** The regressor is a weekly scalar built from an *attention/velocity* metric, not a demand/valuation metric. A stock can react to a trend that isn't economically material, or to one already fully priced by the sector. That confound is handled by the peer-basket control — which is exactly why the control is the decisive test — but the entry's "lower bound" copy gets the direction of that logic wrong (see below).

**What falsifies it.** A negative OOS ablation gain, a circular-rotation placebo that isn't survived, or a non-replicating second window. The entry pre-registered all three, fixed the second window before the key, and builds the null branch as a designed landing, not an apology. The stub run already prints the hard case: −0.31 pts, 52% of names, interval crosses zero. That is the right posture for a measurement instrument.

**Does the instrument establish the claim?** Not on today's data — and more deeply: it may not be *able to* at this sample size. 106 aligned weekly returns, 70/36 OOS split with 8-week circular blocks means roughly **4–5 effective independent blocks in the test window**. **What the product is worth if negative.** More than the numbers suggest: a pre-registered, BH-corrected, two-window-replicated negative result that a research desk can cite; a vendor-free sector-peer exposure model; and an alerts feed where every suppressed name prints its reason.

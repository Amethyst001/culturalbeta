### 1. Is the observed-spread power figure the right criterion?
The criterion is: `informative = power at the observed sd ≥ 80%`

This is actually a reasonable criterion but has a subtle problem. The power at the observed spread being 0% means the test cannot distinguish the observed heterogeneity from noise. However, this is circular in a sense - they're saying "we can't detect what we observed, so the test is uninformative."

The alternative would be to report power at some principled effect size (e.g., the minimal practically significant effect). But the observed spread IS the effect size they measured, so using it as the benchmark makes sense.

Actually, I think there's a deeper issue: the criterion conflates two things:


- Whether the test has power against the null
- Whether the observed effect is real

A test can be uninformative because it's underpowered relative to the observed effect, OR because the observed effect is actually just noise. The criterion doesn't distinguish these. The right question is whether the test is calibrated correctly (null coverage) and what the power would be at a meaningful effect size. Here, the test IS calibrated (rotation null gives correct coverage), but power at the observed spread is 0%. This means the test cannot distinguish the observed spread from noise, which is the correct interpretation.

The benchmark reconciliation shows mean se² = 1,810 versus cross-sectional variance = 1,352, a 25% overshoot. The team attributes this to the analytic benchmark's independence assumption. But this mismatch could stem from several sources: the independence assumption itself, correlation between names' cultural betas, or the rotation null not properly matching the real data structure. Since the rotation null is the test's reference distribution, the analytic figure is indeed miscalibrated for this purpose.

The verdict rule requires positive OOS ΔR², a refit interval clear of zero, and every null at p ≤ 0.10. The test fails on all three counts, so NO SIGNAL is the correct grade. The real question is whether this rule would suppress a genuine effect. With 80 names and ~100 weeks of data, the test has limited power to detect small effects. The rule is conservative by design, which is appropriate for a pre-registered falsification attempt. NO SIGNAL is distinguishable from FAILS because FAILS requires a measured negative effect with a clear interval, whereas NO SIGNAL means the effect is too small to detect. The desk should clearly communicate this distinction.

The quality of the idea is strong conceptually, but the evidence doesn't support the claim. No fatal objections exist—the team has been transparent about limitations. The main concern is whether the copy clearly distinguishes NO SIGNAL from FAILS.

The overstatement to address is "the factor was looked for and not detectable at this window length" in the desk copy. The block permutation null at p 0.830 is the strictest test, but the power analysis reveals the test has essentially no power to detect the observed effect size, which undermines the conclusion. The dispersion test's "uninformative" reading is correct given the 0% power at the observed spread, though the criterion itself is somewhat circular. A more principled approach would use power at a pre-specified minimum detectable effect rather than anchoring on the observed spread. The best next step would be extending the window to increase power, though the team has already noted this is pre-registered.

The headline needs to be rewritten to avoid overstating the findings. Rather than claiming cultural beta is a candidate risk channel, it should reflect that the evidence doesn't support this claim at the current window length. A judge skimming for 60 seconds might miss that the dispersion test has zero power and mistake "NO SIGNAL" for "FAILS" or "UNPOWERED," or conflate the in-sample +0.67 with the out-of-sample -0.05. The core issue is that the test is calibrated correctly but lacks the statistical power to detect the effect size actually observed, making the "uninformative" label appropriate even though it's somewhat circular.
